1. Overview and Purpose 
This project is the capstone practical activity for Part B of COMP 441 (Automation and AI -Augmented 
Testing). Working in assigned groups, students will take a real, mid -sized open -source codebase and 
build a hybrid test suite  combining AI -generated tests with manually authored tests, across unit, 
integration, and acceptance levels while rigorously measuring how effective that suite is using mutation 
testing, coverage analysis, and (depending on the group's track) visual, accessibility, security, or defect -
prediction techniques. 
The project directly operationalizes the module's central theme: AI -generated tests are a useful first 
draft, not ground truth. Every group must produce hard evidence in the form of mutation scores  that 
show where AI-only testing falls short and must close those gaps with human -authored tests. The final 
deliverable is a working, hybrid, human + AI test suite planned with proper risk analysis and traceability, 
partly built test-first, and exercised at multiple test levels , wired into a CI/CD pipeline with an enforced 
quality gate, accompanied by a written report that critically evaluates AI reliability and limitations. 
This brief expands the sample project outline in Section 4.3 of the Module Guide into a full practical 
specification, including differentiated tracks for different groups, a phased timeline aligned to the weekly 
syllabus, detailed task requirements, and a marking rubric. It has been deliberately designed so that, 
together with the module's quizzes and final examination, it gives direct, gradable evidence against every 
Module Learning Outcome in Section 3 of the Module Guide; see the coverage map in Section 1.1.

2. Group Formation and Roles 
Groups of 4 –5 students will be formed in the Week 5 lab session, either by self -selection (subject to 
coordinator sign-off to balance group sizes) or by coordinator allocation where numbers require it. Each 
group must nominate students to the following rot ating-but-accountable roles. In smaller groups of 4, 
the Documentation Lead role may be combined with the Test Lead role. 
Role Primary Responsibilities 
Test Lead / Coordinator Owns the overall test strategy, delegates tasks, tracks milestone 
deadlines, and chairs weekly stand-ups. 
Automation & CI/CD Engineer Sets up the automation framework, Page Object Model structure,

AI Test Engineer Owns prompt design for AI-generated tests, tool comparison, and 
coverage/mutation benchmarking of AI output (Weeks 6–7).

define accountability, not an exclusive division of labor. A confidential peer -contribution form is 
submitted alongside the final report (see Section 9) and factors into the individual mark adjustment 
described in Section 8.

3. Track Assignment — Different Scenarios for Different Groups 
To keep benchmarking meaningful and prevent groups from converging on identical answers, each group 
is assigned (or selects, first -come-first-served, at coordinator discretion) one of five scenario tracks. 
Every track follows the same phased process and rubric (Sections 6–8), but the domain, risk profile, and 
the specialized testing stream in Week 8/12 differ, as shown below. Where the cohort is large, multiple 
groups may share a track but must select different repositories. 
Track A — E-Commerce / Retail Web Application 
Domain focus: shopping cart, checkout, pricing, and inventory logic. Typical stack: JavaScript/TypeScript 
(Node.js/Express or React front end). 
• Specialized stream (Week 8): visual regression testing of the storefront UI and an axe -core 
accessibility audit of the checkout flow — these are mandatory deliverables for this track. 
• Suggested candidate repositories (verify size/license before selecting): a headless commerce engine 
such as Medusa, an open -source storefront template, or a “ Real-world” reference e -commerce 
implementation. 
• Distinctive risk to probe: boundary values on pricing, discounts, and stock quantities; state-transition 
testing of an order's lifecycle (cart → checkout → paid → shipped). 
Track B — FinTech / Banking Transaction Engine 
Domain focus: account balances, transfers, interest calculation, and transaction validation. Typical stack: 
Java or Python. 
• Specialized stream (Week 12): a security-first SAST pass with Semgrep/CodeQL and OWASP-Top-10-
informed threat modelling of the transaction endpoints — mandatory for this track. 
• Suggested candidate repositories: an open -source ledger, double -entry bookkeeping library, or a 
teaching banking-simulation project. 
• Distinctive risk to probe: rounding and currency-precision boundary values, decision-table coverage 
of overdraft/interest rules, and mutation testing rigor on arithmetic operators (a classic weak spot 
for AI-generated tests). 
Track C — Healthcare / Patient-Records Data Validation System 
Domain focus: patient intake forms, appointment scheduling, and data -validation rules. Typical stack: 
Python (Django/Flask) or Java. 
• Specialized stream (Week 10): a defect-prediction classifier (scikit-learn) trained on the repository's 
commit/churn history, plus a short discussion of bias risk in flagging “risky” modules or contributors 
— mandatory for this track. 
• Suggested candidate repositories: an open -source clinic/appointment management system or a 
health-data validation/FHIR-adjacent utility library. 
• Distinctive risk to probe: equivalence partitioning and boundary value analysis on patient data fields 
(dates of birth, dosages, identifiers); data -privacy-aware test data generation (no real PII in test 
fixtures). 
Track D — Developer Tooling / API or CLI Library

Domain focus: a general-purpose library, SDK, or command -line tool with a public API contract. Typical 
stack: Python, Go, or JavaScript/TypeScript. 
• Specialized stream (Week 6–7, deepened): property-based testing with Hypothesis (Python) or fast-

• Suggested candidate repositories: a parsing/validation library, a data -transformation utility, or a

• Distinctive risk to probe: input-space edge cases (empty, null, malformed, very large inputs) and API-

Track E — AI/LLM-Integrated Application (Advanced Track) 
Domain focus: an application with at least one prompt -based or model -driven feature (e.g. a chatbot, 
summarizer, or recommendation feature). Typical stack: Python or JavaScript/TypeScript with an LLM 
API integration. 
• Specialized stream (Weeks 10–11, deepened): in addition to the standard mutation -testing stream 
on the deterministic parts of the codebase, this track builds a lightweight evaluation harness 
(promptfoo or DeepEval) with a small golden dataset to check the AI feature itself for hallucination 
and prompt regressions — mandatory for this track. 
• Suggested candidate repositories: an open -source chatbot scaffold, a retrieval -augmented-
generation demo app, or a small content-generation tool. 
• Note: this track carries a higher degree of technical difficulty and is recommended for groups who 
are comfortable with Week 11 content early; it does not replace the individual “Test an AI-Powered 
Feature” assignment (Section 4.2 of the Module Guide), whi ch remains a separate, individually 
graded piece of work. 
• Coordinator note: track allocation should aim for at least one group per track where the cohort size 
allows, to maximize variety for the Week 13 cross-group showcase and peer learning discussion. 
4. Repository Selection Criteria 
Each group selects one repository for their assigned track, subject to written coordinator approval by 
the end of Week 5. A short (half -page) selection justification is submitted as part of the Project Charter 
(Section 6, Week 5 milestone). 
4.1 Mandatory Criteria 
• Mid-sized: roughly 3,000 –30,000 lines of source code (excluding generated code, vendored 
dependencies, and existing tests). Too small trivialises mutation testing; too large is unmanageable 
in the time available. 
• Builds and runs locally without a paid licence or proprietary infrastructure. 
• Written in a language with mature free/open -source tooling from Section 10 of the Module Guide 
(JavaScript/TypeScript, Python, or Java are strongly preferred). 
• Carries a permissive open-source licence (MIT, Apache-2.0, BSD) that allows academic use, forking, 
and modification.

• Hosted on GitHub or GitLab with visible commit history (needed for Week 10 defect -prediction 
features and Week 7 mutation-testing baselines). 
4.2 Preferred, Not Mandatory 
• An existing (even if thin) test suite, so the group can measure an honest “before AI” baseline. 
• Active maintenance history (commits within the last 2 years) for realistic churn/complexity signals.

Two or more groups may not select the same repository within the same track; the coordinator will 
resolve conflicts on a first-approved basis. 
5. Project Workflow briefly 
1. Select track and repository; set up toolchain; agree roles; draft the test plan and risk register (Week 
5). 
2. Establish a manual/baseline understanding of the codebase, run static analysis, and build a 
requirements traceability matrix (Weeks 5–6, reusing Week 1–2 and Week 4 techniques). 
3. Generate an AI baseline test suite and measure its coverage (Week 6). 
4. Run mutation testing on the AI baseline suite and apply AI -guided test prioritisation to expose and 
rank surviving mutants (Week 7). 
5. Author manual unit tests — and, for Track D, property -based tests — to kill the surviving mutants 
and close coverage gaps (Weeks 7–9). 
6. Complete the track -specific specialized testing stream and the cross -track light -touch 
performance/usability checks (Weeks 8, 10 or 12, depending on track). 
7. Write a small number of new/refactored features test -first (TDD) and translate at least three user -
facing behaviours into Gherkin/BDD acceptance scenarios, backed by an integration-level test suite 
(Weeks 8–9). 
8. Run exploratory testing with an AI co-pilot to catch what scripted tests miss (Week 9). 
9. Re-run mutation testing on the final hybrid suite and record the improvement (Week 11). 
10. Wire the full hybrid suite into a CI/CD pipeline with an enforced quality gate; finalize the written 
report (Weeks 12–12). 
11. Present findings to the class and demonstrate the live pipeline (Week 12).

7.1 Test Plan, Risk Register and Estimation (Week 5) 
Before any tooling work begins, produce a short planning package as part of the Project Charter. This 
closes the module's test-planning and risk-analysis outcome, which the sample brief in the Module Guide 
did not previously require. 
• One-page test plan in IEEE-829-style structure: scope, objectives, methodology, schedule, resource 
allocation (who does what), and entry/exit criteria for the project as a whole. 
• Risk register: identify at least 5 project risks (technical or schedule-related, e.g. “repository too large 
to mutation-test in the time available”), rate each by probability and impact, and state a mitigation 
for each. 
• Estimate the effort for the mutation -testing and hybrid -suite phases using one named estimation 
technique from Week 4 (e.g. three -point estimation or expert judgement), and briefly justify the 
estimate. 
7.2 Requirements Traceability Matrix (Weeks 5–6) 
Reusing the Week 4 traceability technique, derive a short requirements list for the module(s) under test 
and track it through to the test suite as it grows. 
• Identify at least 10 requirements or user -facing behaviours for the selected module(s), drawn from 
the repository's README, issue tracker, or by reverse -engineering behaviour from the code (state 
which source was used for each). 
• Build a requirements traceability matrix (RTM) mapping each requirement to the test case(s) that 
verify it, spanning unit, integration, and acceptance tests as the suite is built out across Weeks 6–12. 
• Update and resubmit the RTM as an appendix to the final report, with any requirement that ends up 
untested clearly flagged and explained. 
7.3 Static Analysis Baseline, incl. Symbolic Analysis (builds on Week 2) 
• Run a static analysis tool (e.g. , SonarQube Community Edition, ESLint/Pylint, or an LLM code 
reviewer) across the selected repository. 
• Report at least: cyclomatic complexity hot-spots, maintainability index, and the top 10 findings 
triaged by severity. 
• Briefly compare traditional linter output against an LLM -based code review of the same file, noting 
agreements and disagreements. 
• Symbolic-analysis discussion (required, short): pick one function with non -trivial branching and 
reason through what a symbolic -execution tool (e.g. conceptually, or using a lightweight tool such 
as CrossHair for Python) would explore that example -based un it tests might miss — a half -page 
discussion is sufficient; a working symbolic-execution run is welcome but not mandatory. 
7.4 AI-Generated Baseline Suite, Mutation Testing & AI-Guided Prioritization (Weeks 
6, 7, 11) 
• Use at least one free/open -source or free -tier AI coding assistant (Continue.dev + Ollama, 
Claude/ChatGPT free tier, or GitHub Copilot free tier) to generate a unit test suite for the selected 
module(s); document the exact prompts used (prompt-engineering log).

• Measure statement and branch coverage of the AI -generated suite, and critically assess assertion 
quality — flag any tests that merely assert “no exception thrown” or check outputs too loosely to be 
meaningful. 
• Run a mutation-testing tool appropriate to the language (PIT for Java, Stryker for JS/TS, mutmut or 
cosmic-ray for Python) and compute the mutation score of the AI -only baseline suite (v1, Week 7) 
and the final hybrid suite (v2, Week 11). 
• For at least 8 surviving mutants in the v1 run, explain in plain language why the AI -generated tests 
failed to catch them, and show the specific manual test written to kill each one. 
• AI-guided test prioritisation (required): using either an ML classifier trained on churn/complexity 
(scikit-learn) or a documented risk -based heuristic, produce a ranked test -execution order for the 
hybrid suite, and justify why it would reduce time-to-detect-a-regression in CI. 
• Present the v1 → v2 mutation-score improvement as a labelled chart in the final report. 
7.5 Hybrid Unit Test Suite Construction (Weeks 7–9) 
• Manually author unit tests to close the gaps identified by mutation testing and by manual code 
reading, using the appropriate test -design techniques from Week 3 (equivalence partitioning, 
boundary value analysis, decision tables, or state transition testing) as justification for each new test, 
not guesswork. 
• Track D groups additionally add property -based tests (Hypothesis/fast -check) for at least two 
functions with a well-defined input domain. 
• Maintain a simple traceability note linking each manually added test back to the mutant(s) or 
coverage gap it addresses and reflect this in the RTM (Section 7.2). 
7.6 Test-Driven Development, BDD, and Integration/Acceptance Testing (Weeks 8–9) 
This closes the module's Agile/DevOps, TDD, and multi-level testing outcome, which was previously only 
touched at the unit level. 
• Test-Driven Development log: pick at least 3 small new features, bug fixes, or refactoring’s to 
implement using a strict red –green–refactor cycle. Keep a short log (timestamped commits or a 
table) showing the failing test written first, the minimal code that made it pass, and the refactor 
step. 
• Behavior-Driven Development: translate at least 3 user -facing behaviors into Gherkin syntax 
(Given/When/Then). Automate them with a BDD framework appropriate to the language (Cucumber 
for Java, pytest-bdd or behave for Python, jest-cucumber or Cucumber.js for JavaScript/TypeScript). 
• Integration-level test suite: write at least 5 integration tests that exercise two or more real 
components together (e.g. a service layer against a real or in -memory database, or two internal 
modules interacting), distinct from the unit-level hybrid suite in Section 7.5. 
• Where the BDD scenarios exercise the system end-to-end, they may double as the acceptance-level 
tests required by MLO 2 — state clearly in the report which test level each BDD scenario represents. 
7.7 Exploratory Testing with an AI Co-Pilot (Week 9) 
• Run one time -boxed (45–60 minute) session -based exploratory testing session per group, with a 
written charter.

• Use an LLM as a real -time “testing co -pilot” to suggest exploratory charters, edge cases, and 
negative-path scenarios during the session. 
• Log all findings in a structured session report (charter, duration, areas covered, bugs/anomalies

7.8 Light-Touch Performance & Usability Checks (all groups) + Track-Specific 
Specialised Stream 
Every group, regardless of track, completes a brief performance check and a brief usability check so that 
Appendix B (Performance Testing) and the usability portion of Week 8 are evidenced across the whole 
cohort, not just Track A. This is intentionally sc oped small (roughly half a day of effort) so it does not 
compete with the track-specific deep dive. 
• Performance (light touch, all groups): pick one representative endpoint, function, or CLI command 
and run a short load test with JMeter, Locust, or Gatling (e.g. 20 –50 simulated concurrent users for 
2–5 minutes). Report response time, throughput, and error rate, and note any obvious bottleneck. 
• Usability (light touch, all groups): perform a short heuristic evaluation of one user-facing flow against 
Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics (or the module's usability -guidelines list from Week 8), and list the 
top 3 issues found with severity ratings. 
• Track-specific specialised stream (deep dive, mandatory for the group's assigned track): complete 
the specialised activity described in Section 3 — visual regression + accessibility audit (Track A), 
security SAST + threat modelling (Track B), defect -prediction classifier (Track C), property -based 
testing (Track D), or LLM evaluation harness (Track E). All other tracks are welcome to attempt 
another track's specialised stream as bonus work (up to 5 additional marks, capped at 100% overall), 
but only after their own mandatory stream is complete. 
7.9 Quality Metrics and Reporting (builds on Week 10) 
• Report at least four quality indicators for the final hybrid suite: coverage percentage, mutation score, 
defect density (defects found per KLOC during the project), and RTM completeness (% of 
requirements with at least one passing test). 
• Present these as a simple dashboard (a spreadsheet chart, a Grafana panel, or an equivalent visual) 
in the final report or as a linked artefact. 
7.10 CI/CD Integration and Quality Gate (Week 13) 
• Wire the final hybrid suite — unit, integration, and acceptance/BDD tests — into a CI/CD pipeline 
(GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, or Jenkins) that runs automatically on every push/pull request. 
• Configure a quality gate that fails the build if coverage or mutation score drops below an agreed 
threshold set by the group (state and justify the threshold chosen). 
• Be ready to demonstrate a live, passing pipeline run — and, ideally, one deliberately broken run that 
the gate correctly blocks — during the Week 13 presentation.

9. Final Report Requirements 
Submit one PDF report per group, 7 –10 pages excluding appendices, using a standard 11pt font and 
2.5cm margins. The extra length versus the Module Guide's original 5 –8-page sample brief reflects the 
added planning, traceability, and multi-level testing evidence now required. The report must follow this 
structure: 
1. Executive Summary (half page): track, repository, headline mutation-score improvement. 
2. Methodology: tools used at each phase, with versions. 
3. Test Plan & Risk Register Summary: key entry/exit criteria, top risks and mitigations, estimation 
technique used. 
4. Requirements Traceability Matrix: full table as an appendix, with a short narrative on any gaps. 
5. Static Analysis Findings, including the symbolic-analysis discussion (brief). 
6. AI-Only Baseline Results: coverage, sample prompts, assertion-quality assessment. 
7. Mutation Testing Results: v1 vs. v2 mutation scores, at least 8 annotated surviving -mutant case 
studies, and the AI-guided prioritisation order with justification. 
8. Hybrid Suite Design Rationale: test-design techniques applied and why. 
9. TDD & BDD Evidence: TDD log summary and the Gherkin scenarios with a note on their automation 
and test level. 
10. Integration & Acceptance Testing Summary: what was exercised together and why it matters beyond 
unit tests. 
11. Track-Specific Specialised Stream Findings, plus the cross -track performance and usability check 
results. 
12. Exploratory Testing Findings. 
13. CI/CD Pipeline Summary and quality-gate justification. 
14. Reflection: strengths, risks, and limitations of AI -generated tests observed in this project, and any 
ethical or reliability concerns (bias, hallucinated assertions, over-reliance). 
15. Appendices: full prompt log, RTM, raw coverage/mutation reports, TDD log, Gherkin feature files, 
peer-contribution form (submitted separately/confidentially where the platform allows). 
A one-page Declaration of AI Tool Use must be attached (see Section 10), consistent with Section 7.7 of 
the Module Guide.

11. Academic Integrity and Use of AI Tools 
AI assistance is not just permitted but required for this project ; it is the point of the exercise. However, 
per Section 7.7 of the Module Guide, all analysis write-ups, root-cause explanations of surviving mutants, 
and critical reflections must be the group's own work. AI-generated tests, findings, and code must always 
be reviewed and verified by the group before submission  and never submitted as ground truth. Any AI 
tool use must be transparently disclosed in the Declaration of AI Tool Use. Submitting unreviewed AI 
output as though it were verified, human-checked work is treated as a breach of the module's academic 
integrity policy.
